# Startup Playbook: From Idea to Product-Market Fit

## Idea Validation Frameworks

### Problem/Solution Fit

Before building anything, validate that your problem is real and your solution makes sense. Problem/solution fit means:
- **The problem is painful enough** that people actively seek solutions (willingness to pay or change behavior)
- **Your solution addresses the root cause**, not a symptom
- **Enough people share the problem** to make a business viable

The test: Can you find 10 strangers (not friends/family) who describe your problem in their own words before you mention it?

### Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) Framework

Customers don't buy products — they "hire" them to get a job done. Understanding the job gives you more durable product insights than feature lists.

**Functional jobs**: The practical task ("I need to track my expenses")
**Emotional jobs**: How they want to feel ("I want to feel in control of my money")
**Social jobs**: How they want to be perceived ("I want my team to see me as organized")

To uncover the real job: Interview recent purchasers and recent churners. Ask "What were you trying to accomplish when you [did the thing]?" and "What did you do before this solution?" The gap between old and new reveals the real job.

### Lean Startup Loop

Build → Measure → Learn. The loop should be as tight as possible:

1. **Identify the riskiest assumption** in your business (usually: "People want this" or "People will pay for this")
2. **Design the minimum experiment** that tests it — a landing page, a concierge MVP, a Wizard of Oz prototype
3. **Define success metrics before running the experiment** (avoid confirmation bias)
4. **Run it with real potential customers**, not colleagues
5. **Analyze the data honestly** and update your model

Most startups fail by building too much before testing the critical assumption. Each loop should reduce uncertainty; if it doesn't, the experiment was poorly designed.

## Early Customer Discovery

### Who to Talk To

Target people who:
- Have the problem acutely right now (not "someday")
- Have tried to solve it (bought a competitor, built a workaround, hired someone)
- Have budget or authority to act

Avoid: people who "might" have the problem in the future, and anyone who is too polite to tell you the truth.

### The Mom Test

Proposed by Rob Fitzpatrick: structure interviews so that even your mom can't give you false positives.

**Good questions**: "Walk me through the last time you had this problem." / "How are you currently handling it?" / "What have you already tried?" / "How much time/money does this cost you?"

**Bad questions**: "Would you use a product that...?" / "Do you think people would pay for...?" / "Would this be useful?"

Rules: Talk about their life, not your idea. Ask about specifics, not hypotheticals. Listen more than you talk.

### Discovery Interview Structure (45 minutes)

1. **Warm-up** (5 min): Their role, context, background
2. **Problem exploration** (20 min): Recent experiences with the problem, current workflow, pain level
3. **Solution probing** (10 min): What they've tried, what worked/didn't, what an ideal outcome looks like
4. **Wrap-up** (10 min): Who else has this problem, who should I talk to next

Aim for 20-30 interviews before drawing conclusions. Look for patterns, not individual opinions.

## MVP Definition

An MVP (Minimum Viable Product) is the smallest thing you can build or do to learn whether your core hypothesis is true.

Common MVP types:
- **Landing page**: Test messaging and demand before building anything
- **Concierge**: Manually deliver the service to 5-10 customers; don't automate yet
- **Wizard of Oz**: Product looks automated but humans power it behind the scenes
- **Smoke test**: Offer the product before it exists; measure signups/purchases
- **Single-feature product**: Build just the core value-delivery mechanism

The MVP question: "What's the minimum feature set that lets a real customer complete the primary job?" Not minimum for you to be proud of — minimum for them to experience the core value.

## Product-Market Fit (PMF) Indicators

PMF is when your product is so well-suited to a market that it pulls customers to it, rather than you having to push.

### Qualitative signals
- Users are upset when you suggest removing a feature
- Customers use the product in ways you didn't anticipate
- Word-of-mouth referrals happen without incentive
- Sales cycles shorten over time
- You can't keep up with inbound demand

### Quantitative signals
- **Retention cohorts flatten** above 20-30% (users keep coming back)
- **Net Promoter Score** (NPS) consistently above 40
- **Sean Ellis test**: >40% of users say they'd be "very disappointed" if the product disappeared
- **Organic growth** (k-factor > 0) — each cohort brings new users
- **Payback period shrinking** as word-of-mouth lowers CAC

### Common PMF mistakes
- Confusing early adopter enthusiasm for PMF (early adopters tolerate rough products)
- Measuring usage instead of retention (activation ≠ PMF)
- Declaring PMF too early — wait for cohort data across 3+ months

## The Pivot Decision

When evidence contradicts your core hypothesis, you face a pivot decision. Types of pivots:

- **Customer segment pivot**: Keep the solution, change who you're targeting
- **Problem pivot**: Keep the customer, solve a different problem for them
- **Platform pivot**: Change from product to platform (or vice versa)
- **Channel pivot**: Change how you reach customers
- **Value capture pivot**: Change how you monetize

Before pivoting, ask: Is the data conclusive? Have we truly exhausted this hypothesis? What specific evidence would make us confident we're right or wrong?
